    What is statistics?

    A discipline, a set of methods, the data itself, and the calculated figures. Most importantly: a method of inquiry — a way of thinking.

    Branches of statistics

    Descriptive — summarizing the sample you have (the results section). Inferential — what you can conclude about the population (the discussion section).

    Levels of measurement

    N O I R — nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. The dividing line between interval and ratio is an absolute zero.

    Measures of central tendency

    Mean, median, mode — plus the range. The typical or most representative value of a group of scores.

    Carrying forward

    Central tendency comes back immediately this week — but now paired with its other half, variability. Levels of measurement come back too, because they decide which measure of central tendency you're allowed to use.

    Review, but load-bearing

    Independent and dependent variables

    You'll have met these before — but everything this module does depends on knowing which is which, so let's be precise.

    Independent Variable

    aka the Manipulated Variable

    • Typically the variable the researcher controls
    • Think of it as the intervention
    • Can potentially explain differences in the outcome you're interested in

    Dependent Variable

    aka the Measured Variable

    • The variable whose levels are observed
    • The outcome
    • Something the researcher measures but doesn't directly control

    An example from clinical work

    Say you're studying levels of depression and you're using the Beck Depression Inventory. The BDI scores are your dependent variable. You control how you administer the BDI — but you can't control what people score. That's the distinction.

    Why it matters right now

    When you ask “is my data normally distributed?”, you are asking it about the dependent variable. Always. That's the outcome, and the outcome is what your results are made of.

    The normal distribution

    Q: How is my data distributed?

    Where it came from

    It's more or less a three-way tie between Abraham de Moivre, Carl Friedrich Gauss, and Pierre-Simon Laplace. That kind of near-simultaneous discovery happened a lot in this period — no social media, no news wire, so people arrived at the same pattern independently and around the same time.

    What they were all doing was measuring natural phenomena — height, the size of apples in an orchard — and they kept finding the same shape: the majority of values hover around a center value.

    The Normal Distribution

    Short explainer

    What the shape gives you

    Mean = median = mode

    When data is distributed normally, all three land in the same place. And the curve has exactly one mode.

    It's symmetric

    Draw a vertical line at the mean and you get two mirror images.

    The mean tells you where

    Know the mean and you know the center — the proverbial top of the bell.

    The SD tells you how wide

    Know the standard deviation and you know how spread out the curve is. Larger SD, wider bell.

    Everyday examples

    Measure the height of everyone at your university and most people are of average height — most values sit in the middle, with the very tall and very short at the extremes. The same happens with shoe size, and with IQ.

    Part 1 · Diagnosing normality

    Reading the curve

    The center is the mean. Everything else is measured in standard deviations away from it.

    The normal curveClick a band
    Click any shaded band to see what proportion of your data falls there.

    How to read the numbers

    • 34.1% of values lie within one standard deviation above the mean, and 34.1% within one standard deviation below.
    • 13.6% lie between one and two standard deviations above; another 13.6% between one and two below.
    • Negative numbers always mean below the mean. Positive numbers mean above.
    The two numbers to remember

    68.2% of all values lie within one standard deviation of the mean.
    95.4% of all values lie within two standard deviations of the mean.

    Made concrete

    Measure the height of a thousand people. About 950 of them will fall within two standard deviations of the mean. Above two standard deviations you have the really, really tall people; below, the really, really short. Those tails are your outliers.

    Which is the whole idea: in nature, things tend toward an average, and the numbers hover around that average — when the data is normally distributed.

    Not all data is normal

    Sometimes the hump slides off to one side and the data is skewed.

    Activity · Left skew or right skew?Follow the tail
    The trick that always works

    Which direction is the tail going? Tail points left, it's left skewed. Tail points right, it's right skewed. That's it — don't overthink the hump.

    The classic example

    Income. The majority of incomes sit within a certain range, and then you have a handful of extremely wealthy outliers pulling one tail out a long way. That's a skewed distribution, and it's why the mean income and the median income of a city can be so far apart.

    Choosing a measure of central tendency

    There are pros and cons to each — but there are also standard, expected ways of using them.

    Mean

    Best choice when there are no extreme values. Very popular, used constantly in psychology research.

    Median

    Best choice when there are extreme values. Use it with rank-ordered variables and whenever outliers are present.

    Mode

    Best for identifying the most characteristic value. Use it with nominal variables.

    Why outliers matter

    Extreme values drag the mean up or down. Imagine the average income in Philadelphia is $50,000 — a million people earning around that. Now add a thousand people earning $5 or $10 million. Fold those into the average and suddenly it looks like the typical Philadelphian earns $80,000.

    Nobody's income changed. The measure just stopped describing the typical person. That's why income is almost always reported as a median.

    Matching the measure to the level of measurement

    Use thisWith this kind of dataWhy
    MeanRatio / IntervalEqual-interval variables — the arithmetic is meaningful
    MedianOrdinalRank-ordered variables, and any distribution with outliers
    ModeNominalCategories can't be averaged or ranked — you can only count them

    Center and spread

    Central tendency is only half the story. The other half is variability — how spread out or close together the scores are.

    Standard Deviation (SD)

    Describes the variability of the data. Small SD, scores clustered tight around the mean. Large SD, scores scattered wide.

    The relationship in one sentence

    The greater the variability, the less accurately your data is summarized by its measure of central tendency. High variability means low predictability.

    The mean tells you what to expect. The standard deviation tells you how much you can trust that expectation. You always report them together, and that's why.

    Part 1 · Diagnosing normality

    Kurtosis

    Different words for differently-shaped distributions. Kurtosis is about the spread — so it's the standard deviation, showing up in the shape of the curve.

    Three shapesClick each

    Remembering platykurtic

    The curve looks like an upside-down plate — wide, low, flat. Big standard deviations, lots of variability.

    Remembering leptokurtic

    Think leaping leprechaun — you'd have to leap really high to get over the hump. Small standard deviations, very little variability.

    Diagnosing normality

    Two things you check: kurtosis — how spread out the data is, whether the hump is a rolling hill or a steep peak — and skewness — whether the data leans in a direction rather than sitting symmetric.

    Part 2 · Hypothesis testing

    Null and alternative hypotheses

    Two things you need to know, and one surprising fact about which one you actually test.

    HA  Alternative Hypothesis

    A statement about your research question. Essentially just your research question turned from a question into a claim.

    H0  Null Hypothesis

    Do the opposite. Whatever your alternative says, the null says it isn't so.

    You'll see these written as H-sub-zero and H-sub-A. People say them out loud as “H-nought,” “H-zero,” or just “the null.”

    Worked example

    Research questionHow does caffeine consumption impact productivity?
    HACaffeine consumption impacts productivity.
    H0Do the opposite: caffeine consumption does NOT impact productivity.
    The part that feels backwards

    Statistics is always testing the null hypothesis. Not your hypothesis — its opposite. Typically we think there is an effect; the null says there's no effect; and the null is what actually goes on trial.

    The p value

    So how do we know whether the null hypothesis holds up? We look at the p value.

    p < .05

    Significant

    p ≥ .05

    Not significant

    .05 is the benchmark cutoff in psychology. You'll read articles where someone sets a stricter benchmark — .01, say — and that's a personal decision they're making. But the bare minimum in the field is that it has to be less than .05.

    The cruel ones

    p = .05 is not significant — the rule is less than .05, and .05 is not less than .05. Neither is .051. Neither is .05000001, no matter how many zeros you put in there. Close, but no cigar.

    Part 2 · Hypothesis testing

    What p < .05 actually means

    The formal statement

    p < .05 means that if the null hypothesis were true, we would expect to see results as extreme as the ones we observed less than 5 times out of 100 due to random chance.

    In terms of the caffeine study

    If caffeine truly had no effect on productivity, we'd see results like ours in fewer than 5 out of every 100 experiments just by chance. That's rare enough that we stop believing the "no effect" explanation.

    So we conclude…

    The null hypothesis doesn't hold up. Caffeine consumption does impact productivity — our alternative hypothesis is supported.

    What it is not

    p is not the probability that your hypothesis is right. It's the probability of getting data like yours in a world where nothing is going on.

    Part 2 · Hypothesis testing

    Reject, or fail to reject

    In statistics we don't get to say a hypothesis is simply true or false. We have to use two very specific phrases.

    p < .05

    REJECT the null hypothesis

    We're rejecting the claim that caffeine does not affect productivity. This is what we're hoping for.

    p ≥ .05

    FAIL TO REJECT the null hypothesis

    We don't have enough evidence to rule out "no effect." Notice we say fail to reject — not accept.

    Why the awkward phrasing

    Failing to reject doesn't prove the null is true — it means you didn't find enough evidence against it. Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. It's a little annoying and a little confusing, and it's also the entire reason the phrase exists.
